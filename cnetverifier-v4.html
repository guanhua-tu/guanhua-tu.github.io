  • M911-Verifier: Uncovering Problematic Designs Hindering Ubiquitous Cellular Emergency Services Access [MobiCom'24; invited to brief the FCC] (Paper)

    • Cellular networks provide the most accessible emergency (9-1-1) services with ubiquitous coverage. To keep them available to everyone, the FCC requires carriers to deliver all 9-1-1 calls to a Public Safety Answering Point (PSAP), whether or not the caller is their subscriber, and 3GPP allows phones to access emergency services across heterogeneous networks: 5G/4G/3G base stations, Wi-Fi, and different carriers. However, this comprehensive support is a double-edged sword. The network functions involved are numerous and interact in complex ways, making emergency services prone to errors, yet these emergency-specific designs remain largely unexplored.

    We develop M911-Verifier, the first emergency-specific model checking tool for uncovering 3GPP design flaws that lead to emergency service failures or delays. It models each protocol as a pair of finite state machines at the phone and in the network, correlates message loss with signal strength rather than using a constant loss rate, and adaptively models diverse emergency scenarios, covering stationary and mobile users, home and visited carriers, and subscribed and anonymous (SIM-less) phones. To tame state explosion across radio access technologies, network generations, and carriers, it decomposes models by procedure, loads checkers on demand, and combines verification approaches; to analyze the resulting counterexamples efficiently, we trained a machine-learning classifier on the root causes of 100 initial samples, which flags counterexamples with unseen root causes for manual inspection.

    M911-Verifier identifies 11 previously unreported design defects in three categories: problematic network selection for initiating emergency calls, emergency-unaware 9-1-1 call operation, and network escalation forbidden during emergency calls; nine of them were validated with three U.S. carriers and two Taiwanese carriers using commodity smartphones. The findings are counterintuitive: despite sufficient wireless coverage, emergency calls may take minutes to connect, fail, or drop, and can perform far worse than regular calls. For example, because 3GPP prohibits emergency calls over Wi-Fi whenever any cellular network is available, 90% of indoor emergency calls failed to reach PSAPs within two minutes at locations with weak cellular but good Wi-Fi signals, while non-emergency calls connected in just 5.85 seconds. Because subscribed phones must prioritize their home carrier for emergency services, paying subscribers may take over a minute to connect, while phones without a SIM card can use any carrier and connect in about four seconds. We propose standards-compliant solutions, namely non-prioritized network selection, an emergency-aware NAS protocol, and unrestricted handover for call continuity, and our prototype reduces emergency call setup times from over a minute to about four seconds. All experiments were conducted responsibly: no emergency call was ever delivered to a PSAP.

    • Overview of M911-Verifier

    • Problematic network selection (Preferring cellular over Wi-Fi results in minutes of emergency call setup delay)

    • Subscribed phones prioritize their home carrier for dialing an emergency call, whereas anonymous phones do not

    • Subscribed phones suffer longer emergency call setup times than anonymous phones

    • Network escalation forbidden during emergency calls (A call dropped due to a forbidden 3G-to-4G handover)

    • Proposed solution (Emergency call setup time with and without our solution)

  • CNetVerifer: An Automated, Extensible Control-Plane Protocols Verication Technique[SIGCOMM'14, IEEE/ACM ToN'15]

    • We develop CNetVerifier, an automated control-plane protocol interaction verification method with domain-specific heuristics and model checking techniques to systematically explore possible problematic interactions in cellular networks. The main advantage of this methodology is the exploration of problems/issues in mobile networks is not restricted by unlimited use scenarios (time consuming) or the lack of full access to mobile device or network infrastructure. Its impact on the wireless/mobile networking technology and cybersecurity can be considered as far-reaching. It does not only capture the fundamental design issues of mobile networks span over multiple dimensions, but also discover the security loopholes> caused by improper designs of control-plane protocols of mobile networks. The lessons we learnt can be even applied to the next big things (e.g., 5G, Internet of Thing, Device-to-Device communication) in the networking area.
